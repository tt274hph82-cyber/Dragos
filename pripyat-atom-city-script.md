# Company Town — Episode 6
# Pripyat: The Soviet Atom-City

**Target length:** 16–19 min (~2,500 words narration at ~140 wpm)
**Tone:** calm, investigative, human — not horror, not "creepy abandoned place"

## Packaging

**Title options (test 2–3):**
1. The Perfect Soviet Town Built for a Nuclear Plant (Then It Died in 36 Hours)
2. Pripyat Wasn't a Ghost Town. It Was a Company Town.
3. The USSR Built Cities for Its Reactors. This Is What Happened to Them.

**Thumbnail:** Split image — left: colorful 1970s Pripyat street with people (archive, warm tones); right: same angle today, grey and overgrown. Text (max 3 words): **"BUILT FOR A REACTOR"**

**Why this angle:** "Pripyat" alone is heavily covered by large channels. The differentiator is the *atomgrad system*: Pripyat as one of a family of towns, plus what happened to its "sister" cities (Slavutych, Energodar, Visaginas). That's content almost nobody covers.

## SCRIPT

### 0:00 — Cold open (hook)

[VISUAL: Archive color footage of Pripyat — young families, strollers, wide boulevards]

On the morning of April 27th, 1986, Pripyat was one of the youngest cities in the Soviet Union. The average resident was around 26 years old. The streets were full of strollers. The shops were better stocked than in most Soviet cities.

[VISUAL: Slow push-in on the Ferris wheel today]

By that evening, almost fifty thousand people were gone. They were told to pack for three days. None of them ever came back to live there.

Most videos tell you Pripyat is a ghost town. But that's not really what it was. Pripyat was a company town. It was built for one employer, one purpose and one machine. And it wasn't the only one.

The Soviet Union built a whole family of these cities. They were called *atomgrads*, atom-cities. This is the story of the most famous one, and of the sisters it left behind.

[TITLE CARD: COMPANY TOWN — "The Atom-City"]

---

### 1:15 — Part 1: A city with one employer

[VISUAL: Map zooming into northern Ukraine, the Pripyat River, the plant location]

In the late 1960s, the Soviet Union decided to build a nuclear power station in the marshy forests of northern Ukraine, near the old town of Chernobyl. A power plant needs thousands of workers, and those workers need somewhere to live.

So, on February 4th, 1970, construction began on a brand-new town about three kilometres from the plant site. It was named after the river that ran beside it: Pripyat.

[VISUAL: Archive construction photos, prefab panel blocks]

This is the key to understanding everything that came later. Pripyat didn't grow the way normal towns grow. There was no market square that slowly became a city over centuries. It was designed on paper, all at once, around a single question: *what does a nuclear plant need?*

It needed engineers, operators, construction crews, and their families. So the city was built for them, and almost everyone there was, directly or indirectly, working for the plant.

In the West, we'd recognise the model: Hershey had chocolate, Pullman had railway cars, and Pripyat had a reactor.

---

### 3:00 — Part 2: The atomgrad blueprint

[VISUAL: Animated map of the USSR with dots appearing: Sosnovy Bor, Desnogorsk, Kurchatov, Udomlya, Polyarnye Zori, Energodar, Visaginas]

Pripyat wasn't a one-off. Across the Soviet Union, nearly every major nuclear plant came with its own satellite town. Sosnovy Bor served the Leningrad plant, Desnogorsk the Smolensk plant and Kurchatov the Kursk plant. Energodar in southern Ukraine was built for what would become the largest nuclear plant in Europe, and Sniečkus, later renamed Visaginas, served the Ignalina plant in Lithuania.

They followed a similar formula:
- A location close to the plant, but upwind and across water where possible
- Standardised prefab apartment blocks
- A "Palace of Culture" for concerts and films
- Sports complexes, swimming pools, cinemas
- A population that was young, educated, and relatively well paid

[VISUAL: Side-by-side of Pripyat and Energodar apartment blocks — nearly identical]

Working in nuclear energy was prestigious. These towns were showcases, a little preview of the Soviet future. Supplies were often better than in ordinary cities. For a young engineer in the 1970s, getting a flat in an atomgrad was a real step up.

---

### 5:00 — Part 3: Life in Pripyat

[VISUAL: Archive photos — Lenin Avenue, Hotel Polissya, Palace of Culture "Energetik", Azure swimming pool]

Pripyat received official city status in 1979. By the mid-1980s it had around 49,000 residents.

It had schools, kindergartens, a hospital, a hotel, a palace of culture, a stadium, an indoor swimming pool called Azure, a river port and a large supermarket. Because the residents were so young, the city's birth rate was very high. Some accounts describe roughly a thousand children being born there every year.

[VISUAL: Archive home-movie style footage, families on the riverbank]

People who grew up there remember it fondly: the river beaches in summer, the forests and the sense that they were living somewhere modern and important.

The plant kept growing too. By 1983, four reactors were running, and two more were under construction. Pripyat was planned to grow with them, and new districts were already on the drawing board.

But a company town has a weakness built into its foundation. When everything depends on one employer, everything is exposed to that one employer. In most company towns the danger is economic: the factory closes, the jobs disappear and the town slowly empties.

In Pripyat, the danger was physical, and it was three kilometres away.

---

### 7:15 — Part 4: April 26, 1986

[VISUAL: Slow, respectful — night sky, the plant silhouette, no sensational graphics]

At 1:23 a.m. on April 26th, 1986, during a safety test, Reactor No. 4 at the Chernobyl plant exploded.

We won't go deep into the technical failure here; there are excellent videos that do. What matters for our story is what the city *didn't* know.

[VISUAL: Archive photos of normal life — Saturday street scenes]

April 26th was a Saturday. In Pripyat, life mostly carried on. Children went to school, which held Saturday classes in the USSR. People were outside and some couples got married. The city's new amusement park, with its yellow Ferris wheel, was getting ready to open for the May Day holiday on May 1st.

The workers at the plant knew something was badly wrong. Many of the people who lived in Pripyat *were* those workers and their families. But official information was minimal, and the people in charge hesitated over the decision to evacuate.

---

### 9:00 — Part 5: Thirty-six hours

[VISUAL: Archive evacuation footage — long lines of buses]

On the afternoon of April 27th, around 36 hours after the explosion, a radio announcement went out. Residents were told they would be temporarily evacuated, and to take documents, essentials and food for a few days.

More than a thousand buses arrived from Kyiv and the surrounding region. Within a few hours, a city of nearly fifty thousand people had been emptied.

[VISUAL: Pause on photo of an empty street, hold 3 seconds in silence]

Think about what that means for a company town. In Pullman or Fordlandia, decline took years. People had time to sell their homes and say goodbye. In Pripyat, the employer that had created the city also ended it in a single afternoon.

"Three days" became forever. The flats, the toys, the photos and the furniture all stayed where they were.

---

### 10:45 — Part 6: The company didn't leave

[VISUAL: Map showing the exclusion zone, the plant still operating]

Here's the part most people don't realise. When the residents left, the *employer* didn't.

The Chernobyl plant's other reactors went back into operation. The last one wasn't shut down until December 2000. The plant still needed thousands of workers, but the workers no longer had a home.

For a while, the swimming pool in Pripyat stayed open for the clean-up workers, known as liquidators, but the city itself could never be lived in again.

So the Soviet Union did something remarkable. It built a *new* company town.

---

### 12:00 — Part 7: Slavutych, the replacement city

[VISUAL: Drone/photo footage of Slavutych — distinctive, colourful districts]

About 50 kilometres east, outside the most contaminated areas, construction began on a city called Slavutych.

And it was built in an unusual way. Different Soviet republics each designed and built their own district. There's a Tallinn quarter, a Riga quarter, a Vilnius quarter, a Baku quarter, a Yerevan quarter, a Tbilisi quarter, as well as districts from Moscow, Leningrad, Belgorod and Kyiv. Each one has its own architectural style.

[VISUAL: Side-by-side of Baltic-style and Caucasian-style blocks in Slavutych]

It was the last city ever built by the Soviet Union, and in a way it was Pripyat's second life. Many former Pripyat residents and plant workers moved there, and it was just as young.

And it faced the same problem: one city, one employer. When the plant fully closed in 2000, Slavutych had to figure out what a company town does when the company is gone.

---

### 13:45 — Part 8: The sisters today

[VISUAL: Return to the animated atomgrad map]

So what happened to the rest of the atom-city family?

**Visaginas**, in Lithuania, was built for the Ignalina plant. When Lithuania joined the EU, it agreed to shut that plant down, and the last reactor closed in 2009. The town has been shrinking ever since. It's the classic company-town story, slow and economic.

**Energodar**, in southern Ukraine, is home to the Zaporizhzhia plant, the largest in Europe. Since 2022, the town and its plant have been under Russian occupation. It's a reminder that an atom-city's fate is tied to its reactor, and to whoever controls that reactor.

[VISUAL: Sosnovy Bor / Desnogorsk modern photos]

Others, like Sosnovy Bor and Desnogorsk in Russia, still function much as they were designed to, as towns that exist because a plant exists.

---

### 15:15 — Conclusion: What Pripyat really teaches

[VISUAL: Slow drone over overgrown Pripyat, trees growing through Lenin Avenue]

When we look at Pripyat today, we usually see a disaster: radiation, ruins, a frozen moment in 1986.

But look at it the way we look at every town on this channel. Pripyat was a promise: come here, work for us, and we'll give you a modern life. For about sixteen years, that promise was kept.

Every company town makes the same bargain: it gives you everything, but it depends on one thing. Most of the time, that dependence ends slowly. In Pripyat, it ended in one afternoon.

[VISUAL: The Ferris wheel, final shot]

The Ferris wheel was supposed to turn for the first time on May Day. It's still waiting.
